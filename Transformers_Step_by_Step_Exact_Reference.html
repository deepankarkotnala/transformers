0.1 · Orientation

The destination: the whole Transformer in one picture

⏱ About 10 min

Goal: See the complete machine before studying its parts, so every later lesson has an obvious place to fit.

The original Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) was built to translate. Give it the German sentence Ich mag Tee and it produces I like tea, one token at a time. Below is the entire model. Don't try to understand it yet. Each box is labelled with the lesson that explains it, and you can click a box to jump there.

Read the picture in five steps

  1. Text becomes vectors. Each sentence is split into tokens (C1). Each token looks up a learned vector (C2), and a position signal is added to it (D7).
  2. The encoder builds context. Six identical blocks repeat two operations. Self-attention lets each source token gather information from the others (D1–D5). A feed-forward layer then transforms each token on its own (E1).
  3. The decoder reads the target so far. Its self-attention is masked so that a position cannot peek at later target words (D6).
  4. Cross-attention consults the source. Each target position asks the encoder output which source words matter right now (E3).
  5. Scores become a prediction. A linear layer and softmax turn the final vector into a probability for every word in the vocabulary (E4). The model picks a word, appends it, and repeats (E7).

The running example

The whole course uses one sentence pair, so you can compare each new ingredient with what came before:

Lesson A1 · Math you'll actually use

Vectors and the dot product as similarity

⏱ About 15 minNo prerequisites: a good starting point.

Goal: Compute a dot product and read it as a match score. This is the exact operation attention uses to decide where to look.

Attention's first job is to answer one question for every pair of tokens: how well does this one match that one? The Transformer answers it with a single operation, the dot product. If you understand this lesson, half of the attention formula is already familiar.

A vector is a list of numbers, and also an arrow

A vector is an ordered list of numbers, such as a = [2, 1]. With two numbers you can draw it as an arrow from the origin to the point (2, 1). Its length follows Pythagoras: |a| = √(2² + 1²) = √5 ≈ 2.236.

Real token vectors have 512 numbers. We can't draw those, but every idea here works the same way in 512 dimensions.

The dot product: multiply matching entries, then add

a · b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + … + aₙbₙ
[2, 1] · [1, 2] = 2×1 + 1×2 = 4

The result is a single number, not a vector. You'll meet this same calculation in B1 under another name: a neuron's weighted sum is a dot product between inputs and weights.

What the number means

The dot product has a second, geometric formula: a · b = |a| |b| cos θ, where θ is the angle between the arrows. The cosine is 1 when the arrows point the same way, 0 when they are perpendicular and −1 when they are opposite. So the sign and size of the dot product tell you how aligned two vectors are:

ArrowsExampleDot productReading
Same direction[1,0] · [1,0]1strong match
Perpendicular[1,0] · [0,1]0no alignment
Opposite[1,0] · [−1,0]−1anti-match

Dividing by both lengths removes the effect of size and leaves only direction. This is called cosine similarity, and it always lies between −1 and 1. For [2,1] and [1,2] it is 4 ÷ (√5 × √5) = 0.8.

Explore: turn the arrows

Change the angle and length of each arrow. Watch the dot product grow as the arrows align, fall to zero when they are perpendicular, and turn negative when they point apart.

Meet the running example

From here on, the target sentence I like tea uses three toy vectors: I = [1,0], like = [0,1], tea = [1,1]. Here is every dot product between them:

·I [1,0]like [0,1]tea [1,1]
I101
like011
tea112

Two things are worth noticing. First, "like" matches "tea" exactly as well as it matches itself (both scores are 1). Second, tea · tea = 2 is larger than like · like = 1, even though each vector matches itself perfectly in direction. The raw dot product rewards length as well as direction.

Why this matters later

In D2 you'll compute attention for the token "like". Its first step is exactly the middle row of this table: scores [0, 1, 1]. Attention uses the raw dot product, scaled down a little (D4), rather than cosine similarity. So a vector's length affects how strongly it matches, and training learns to use that.

Common mistake

"A high dot product means two words mean the same thing." Not by itself. The dot product measures geometric alignment. It becomes meaningful only because training arranges the vectors so that useful matches end up aligned.

Practice

Compute

[3, −1] · [2, 4] = ? Are these vectors pointing roughly together, apart, or at right angles?

Show the answer

3×2 + (−1)×4 = 6 − 4 = 2. The result is small and positive, so the angle between them is a little less than 90°. Their cosine similarity is 2 ÷ (√10 × √20) ≈ 0.14.

Explain

Why is tea · tea larger than like · like, when both vectors match themselves perfectly in direction?

Compare your explanation

a · a = |a|². The tea vector is longer (√2 versus 1), so its self-match is 2 rather than 1. The dot product mixes direction with length, while cosine similarity keeps only direction.

Key takeaways

  • Dot product = multiply matching entries and add. The result is one number.
  • It is large when vectors align, zero when they are perpendicular and negative when they are opposed: a · b = |a||b|cos θ.
  • Attention scores are dot products between token vectors. Length matters as well as direction.

Lesson A2 · Math you'll actually use

Matrices, shapes and transpose

⏱ About 20 minUses:A1Dot product as similarity

Goal: Read the shapes of arrays and predict the result of a matrix product. You'll use this skill in every attention calculation.

A sentence is several token vectors, and attention compares every token with every other token. Writing those dot products one by one would be slow and error-prone. A matrix lets us compute them all in one operation.

Matrices and shapes

A matrix is a rectangle of numbers. In this course each row is a token position and each column is a feature. The running example stacks its three token vectors into one matrix:

X = [[1, 0], ← I
[0, 1], ← like
[1, 1]] ← tea shape (3, 2): 3 tokens, 2 features

The shape (rows, columns) is the first thing to check in any calculation. A real model's X for a 7-token sentence has shape (7, 512).

Matrix multiplication is many dot products

To multiply A by B, take the dot product of each row of A with each column of B. The entry in row i, column j of the result is row i of A · column j of B.

X = [[1, 2], W = [[1, 0],
[3, 4]] [0, 2]]

XW = [[1×1 + 2×0, 1×0 + 2×2], = [[1, 4],
[3×1 + 4×0, 3×0 + 4×2]] [3, 8]]

Here W keeps the first feature and doubles the second. For the product to exist, the inner dimensions must match, because each dot product needs equal-length vectors. The outer dimensions give the result's shape:

(3 × 4) @ (4 × 2) → (3 × 2)

Read this as: three tokens go in with 4 features each, and three tokens come out with 2 features each. Multiplying by a weight matrix changes the number of features per token. It never changes the number of tokens.

Transpose: turn rows into columns

The transpose Xᵀ swaps rows and columns, so a (3, 2) matrix becomes (2, 3). Why does this matter? Multiply X by its own transpose:

X Xᵀ = (3 × 2) @ (2 × 3) → (3 × 3)

= [[1, 0, 1],
[0, 1, 1],
[1, 1, 2]]

That is exactly the table of all dot products from A1, computed in one step. Row i, column j is token i · token j. Attention's formula contains QKᵀ, and it does the same thing: every query against every key, in one product.

Two more pieces of notation

Elementwise operations. Multiplying two same-shaped matrices entry by entry (written A ⊙ B) is a different operation from matrix multiplication. When a formula writes AB or A·B with matrices, it means the row-by-column product.

Broadcasting. Adding a single vector to every row of a matrix is written X + b. For example, [[1,0],[0,1]] + [10, 20] = [[11,20],[10,21]]. Bias terms and positional encodings (D7) are added this way.

Tensors. A tensor is an array with any number of dimensions. A batch of 2 sentences, each with 3 tokens of 4 features, has shape (2, 3, 4): read it as (examples, tokens, features). Matrix products act on the last two dimensions, separately for each example.

Practice

Compute

X has shape (5, 8) and W has shape (8, 3). What shape is XW? What about Wᵀ?

Show the answer

XW is (5, 3): five tokens, each now with three features. Wᵀ is (3, 8).

Compute

Q has shape (10, 64). What shape is QQᵀ, and what does each entry mean?

Show the answer

(10, 10). Entry (i, j) is the dot product of row i with row j: one score for every pair of the 10 positions.

Explain

Why can't you multiply a (3, 2) matrix by another (3, 2) matrix, and what one change makes it possible?

Compare your explanation

The inner dimensions (2 and 3) don't match, so the rows and columns have different lengths and their dot products are undefined. Transposing the second matrix gives (3, 2) @ (2, 3) → (3, 3).

Key takeaways

  • (a × b) @ (b × c) → (a × c). The inner dimensions must match.
  • A weight matrix changes the features per token, never the number of tokens.
  • X Xᵀ produces every pairwise dot product at once. QKᵀ in attention does the same.

Lesson A3 · Math you'll actually use

Exponentials, logarithms and softmax

⏱ About 20 minUses:A1Dot product as similarity

Goal: Turn any list of scores into positive weights that sum to 1, and predict how scaling the scores changes those weights.

Dot products give raw scores such as [0, 1, 1] or [−2.3, 4.1, 0.7]. Scores like these can be negative and don't add up to anything in particular. To use them as "how much of each", we need positive weights that sum to 1. The Transformer does this conversion in two places: inside attention (D2) and at the very end, where it produces word probabilities (E4).

A quick refresher: e, exp and ln

e ≈ 2.718 is a mathematical constant. exp(x) = eˣ is always positive and grows quickly: exp(0) = 1, exp(1) ≈ 2.718, exp(2) ≈ 7.389, exp(−1) ≈ 0.368. Two properties matter here: exp(a + b) = exp(a) × exp(b), and a bigger input always gives a bigger output.

ln (the natural logarithm) undoes exp: ln(eˣ) = x. Some values: ln(1) = 0, ln(0.5) ≈ −0.693, and ln of a number between 0 and 1 is always negative. Cross-entropy loss (B2) is −ln(p), so you'll see ln again soon.

Softmax in two steps

softmax(s)ᵢ = exp(sᵢ) / Σⱼ exp(sⱼ)
Worked example

Scores s = [2, 1, 0].

Step 1, exponentiate: [e², e¹, e⁰] ≈ [7.389, 2.718, 1.000] total ≈ 11.107
Step 2, divide by total: [0.665, 0.245, 0.090] sum = 1

The largest score gets the largest share, but the others still get something. That is the "soft" in softmax. A hard max would give [1, 0, 0].

Four properties you will rely on

  1. Positive and sums to 1. The outputs can be used directly as weights or probabilities.
  2. Order is preserved. A higher score always gets a higher weight.
  3. Only differences matter. Adding the same number to every score changes nothing: softmax([12, 11, 10]) = softmax([2, 1, 0]). Code exploits this by subtracting the maximum first, which avoids overflow.
  4. Scale controls sharpness. Multiplying the scores spreads them apart and concentrates the weights. Dividing them flattens the weights.
ScoressoftmaxEffect
[1, 0.5, 0] (÷2)[0.506, 0.307, 0.186]flatter
[2, 1, 0][0.665, 0.245, 0.090]baseline
[4, 2, 0] (×2)[0.867, 0.117, 0.016]sharper

Property 4 is the reason attention divides its scores by √dₖ (D4). It is also how "temperature" works when a chatbot samples text (E7).

Explore: softmax and scale

Move the three scores, then move the scale. Watch the weights always sum to 1, and watch large scales push almost all the weight onto the top score.

Common mistake

"Softmax picks the maximum." It doesn't pick anything. It gives every option a share. Attention depends on this: a token can take 50% of its information from one word and 30% from another.

Practice

Compute

softmax([0, 0]) and softmax([0, ln 3]). Hint: e^(ln 3) = 3.

Show the answer

[0.5, 0.5], because equal scores give equal weights. Then exp gives [1, 3], the total is 4, and the weights are [0.25, 0.75].

Explain

Why does subtracting the maximum score before exponentiating not change the result?

Compare your explanation

exp(sᵢ − m) = exp(sᵢ) × exp(−m). Every term, including the total, is multiplied by the same factor exp(−m), so the factor cancels in the division. The point is to keep the numbers small enough that exp doesn't overflow.

Key takeaways

  • Softmax: exponentiate, then divide by the total. The outputs are positive and sum to 1.
  • Only differences between scores matter. Bigger differences give sharper weights.
  • The Transformer uses softmax for attention weights and for next-token probabilities.

Lesson A4 · Math you'll actually use

Derivatives and the chain rule

⏱ About 20 minNo prerequisites: a good starting point.

Goal: Read a derivative as "how much the output moves when I nudge the input", and use the chain rule to derive the gradient formula that B3 uses.

Training a network means adjusting millions of numbers so that a loss goes down. To do that we need, for each number, the answer to one question: if I nudge this parameter up a little, does the loss go up or down, and by how much? That answer is a derivative.

A derivative is a slope

For f(w) = w², nudge w from 3 to 3.01. The output moves from 9 to 9.0601, so the rate of change is 0.0601 ÷ 0.01 ≈ 6.01. Make the nudge smaller and the rate approaches exactly 6. We write f′(3) = 6, or df/dw = 6 at w = 3.

The sign tells you the direction: positive means increasing w increases f, and negative means increasing w decreases f. The size tells you how sensitive f is to w.

Three rules cover almost everything here

RuleExampleDerivative
Powerwⁿn·wⁿ⁻¹ (so w² → 2w)
Constant multiplec·wc (so 3w → 3)
Sumf(w) + g(w)f′(w) + g′(w)

Also: ReLU(z) = max(0, z) has slope 1 when z > 0 and slope 0 when z < 0. At exactly z = 0 the slope is undefined, and frameworks simply choose 0 or 1.

Many inputs: partial derivatives and the gradient

A loss depends on many parameters. A partial derivative ∂L/∂w₁ is the slope with respect to w₁ while every other parameter is held fixed. Collect all of them into one vector and you have the gradient. It points in the direction that increases L fastest, so we step the opposite way.

The chain rule

When one quantity affects another through an intermediate step, multiply the slopes along the chain:

if L depends on u, and u depends on w: dL/dw = dL/du × du/dw
Worked example: the gradient used in B3

A one-weight model predicts wx, and its loss is squared error L = (wx − y)². Introduce the intermediate u = wx − y, so that L = u².

dL/du = 2u (power rule)
du/dw = x (constant multiple; y doesn't depend on w)
dL/dw = 2u · x = 2(wx − y) · x

With x = 2, y = 4 and w = 0 this gives 2(0 − 4)(2) = −16. The negative sign means increasing w lowers the loss. B3 uses exactly this number.

A deep network is a long chain: weight → hidden value → next hidden value → … → loss. Backpropagation (B3) is the chain rule applied systematically from the loss backward, reusing each intermediate product rather than recomputing it.

How much calculus do you need? In practice nobody differentiates a Transformer by hand: deep-learning frameworks compute every gradient automatically (automatic differentiation). You need to know what a gradient means and why the chain rule lets it flow back through many layers. Residual connections (E1) are designed to help exactly that flow.

Compare your explanation

Each link scales a small change. If u moves 3 units per unit of w, and L moves 2 units per unit of u, then a nudge in w is scaled by 3 and then by 2, so L moves 6 units per unit of w. Scalings compound by multiplying.

Key takeaways

  • A derivative is a slope: how far the output moves per small nudge of the input. Its sign gives the direction.
  • The gradient collects the slopes for every parameter. Training steps against it.
  • Chain rule: multiply the slopes along a chain of dependencies. Backpropagation is that rule applied efficiently.

Lesson A5 · Math you'll actually use

Mean, variance and normalization

⏱ About 15 minUses:A1Dot product as similarity

Goal: Compute a mean, variance and standard deviation, normalize a vector, and see why a dot product's typical size grows like √d.

Two pieces of the Transformer are really statistics. Layer normalization (E1) recenters and rescales every token vector. The √dₖ in the attention formula (D4) exists because dot products get bigger as vectors get wider. Both need just three quantities.

Mean, variance and standard deviation

Worked example

Values [2, 4, 6, 8].

mean μ = (2 + 4 + 6 + 8) / 4 = 5
deviations from the mean: [−3, −1, 1, 3]
variance σ² = mean of squared deviations = (9 + 1 + 1 + 9) / 4 = 5
standard deviation σ = √5 ≈ 2.236

The variance measures spread in squared units. The standard deviation is back in the original units: values typically sit about σ away from the mean. (This is the population variance, dividing by n. That is what layer normalization uses.)

Normalizing: mean 0, spread 1

Subtract the mean, then divide by the standard deviation:

(x − μ) / σ: [2, 4, 6, 8] → [−1.342, −0.447, 0.447, 1.342]

The result has mean 0 and standard deviation 1, whatever scale the input had. Layer normalization does exactly this to each token's features, then lets the model rescale with learned parameters. It keeps numbers in a predictable range as they pass through dozens of layers.

Why dot products grow with width

Here is a useful fact: when you add up independent random quantities, their variances add. A dot product q · k = q₁k₁ + q₂k₂ + … + q_dk_d is a sum of d terms. If each qᵢ and kᵢ is random with mean 0 and variance 1, each product qᵢkᵢ has variance 1, so the sum has variance d.

Lesson B1 · Neural networks

Neurons and layers, starting from familiar ML

⏱ About 15 minUses:A1Dot product as similarityA2Matrices, shapes and transpose

Goal: Understand weights, biases, activations and hidden layers, and see that a whole layer is one matrix multiplication.

Start with a model you already know. Suppose a simple regression model estimates delivery time as 2 × distance + 5 × stops + 10. For 3 km and 2 stops it predicts 26 minutes. (The numbers are invented for this example.)

What turns this into a neuron?

Distance and stops are the inputs. The numbers 2 and 5 are weights: they control how strongly each input affects the result. The 10 is a bias, an adjustable offset. Training changes these parameters. Processing a new delivery changes only the inputs.

z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b = w · x + b (a dot product plus a bias, from A1)
neuron output = activation(z)

A neuron computes that weighted sum, then passes it through an activation function. The most common one is ReLU, which keeps positive values and replaces negative values with zero: ReLU(3) = 3, ReLU(−2) = 0.

Why add an activation and more layers?

A network feeds one layer's outputs into the next layer as inputs. A layer in the middle is called a hidden layer. It computes useful features, so we don't have to design every feature by hand.

But stacking plain weighted sums gives you nothing new. If h = 2x + 1 and y = 3h + 4, then y = 6x + 7, and the two layers collapse into one. A nonlinear activation such as ReLU breaks that collapse, which lets the network represent curved, conditional relationships.

A tiny forward pass

Let x = 2. The hidden neuron computes z = 0.5 × 2 − 2 = −1, and ReLU makes its output h = 0. A final linear neuron computes 3h + 1 = 1. This is prediction, not training: no parameter changed.

A layer of neurons is one matrix multiplication

Give the delivery model a second neuron with weights [1, −1] and bias 0. Put each neuron's weights in a column of W:

x = [3, 2] W = [[2, 1], b = [10, 0]
[5, −1]]

xW + b = [3×2 + 2×5, 3×1 + 2×(−1)] + [10, 0] = [16, 1] + [10, 0] = [26, 1]

Both neurons are computed at once: 26 from the first, as before, and 1 from the second. With a batch of deliveries stacked as rows of X, the same line computes every neuron for every delivery: XW + b. This is called a linear layer (also known as a dense or fully connected layer), and it is the most common operation in a Transformer.

X: (n, d_in) @ W: (d_in, d_out) + b: (d_out) → (n, d_out)

A Transformer is a neural network with a particular arrangement of layers. Its feed-forward sublayer (E1) is exactly two linear layers with ReLU between them, widening each token from 512 to 2048 features and back.

Lesson B2 · Neural networks

Measuring error: squared error and cross-entropy

⏱ About 20 minUses:A3Exponentials, logs and softmax

Goal: Compute the loss for a number prediction and for a classification, and understand the loss the Transformer actually minimizes.

A network can't improve unless it has one number that says how bad its current prediction is. That number is the loss. Training is the search for parameters that make the loss small on average.

Predicting a number: squared error

For a numerical target, a simple choice is (prediction − target)². Predicting 26 minutes when the truth is 30 gives a loss of 16. Squaring makes every error positive and punishes large errors heavily.

Predicting a category: logits → softmax → probability

For classification, the network outputs one raw score per class. These scores are called logits. Softmax (A3) turns them into probabilities. Suppose a classifier for {cat, dog, bird} outputs logits [2, 1, 0]:

softmax([2, 1, 0]) = [0.665, 0.245, 0.090] → "cat" with probability 0.665

Cross-entropy: −ln(probability of the right answer)

loss = −ln(p_correct)
Correct classp_correctLoss
cat0.665−ln 0.665 ≈ 0.408
bird0.090−ln 0.090 ≈ 2.408
(perfect)1.0000

A confident, correct model gets a loss near 0. A model that gave the right answer little probability gets a large loss. The loss keeps growing as p approaches 0, so confidently wrong answers are punished most.

A remarkably simple gradient

For softmax followed by cross-entropy, the gradient of the loss with respect to the logits is just p − y, where y is the one-hot vector for the correct class. If the correct class is cat, then y = [1, 0, 0]:

p − y = [0.665 − 1, 0.245, 0.090] = [−0.335, 0.245, 0.090]

Gradient descent moves against this: it raises the cat logit and lowers the others in proportion to the probability each one wrongly took. You don't need to derive this formula, but it explains why this pairing trains so well.

The Transformer's loss

Predicting the next token is classification with one class per vocabulary entry, about 37,000 of them in the paper's English–German setup. At every target position the model outputs 37,000 logits. The loss is −ln of the probability given to the correct next token, averaged over all positions.

Papers often report perplexity = e^(average loss). It's roughly "how many words the model is torn between". A loss of 1.609 gives perplexity e^1.609 ≈ 5. The original paper reports perplexity on its development set.

A right/wrong count is flat almost everywhere: small parameter changes don't change it, so its gradient is zero. Cross-entropy changes smoothly with the probability, which gives a useful gradient even when the answer is already right but not yet confident.

Key takeaways

  • Loss is a single number measuring how bad the predictions are. Training lowers it.
  • Classification: logits → softmax → cross-entropy = −ln(p_correct).
  • The Transformer is a classifier over the vocabulary, applied at every target position.

Lesson B3 · Neural networks

Gradient descent and backpropagation

⏱ About 25 minUses:A4Derivatives and the chain ruleB2Loss functions

Goal: Perform one training update by hand, and follow backpropagation through a two-layer network.

A forward pass produces a prediction with the current weights. Training asks a different question: how should those weights change so that the loss goes down? The answer is to compute the gradient and take a small step against it. Then repeat.

One complete update

Worked example

The model predicts w × x, with x = 2, target 4, and w starting at 0. The prediction is 0 and the loss is (0 − 4)² = 16. From A4, the gradient is 2(wx − y)·x = 2(0 − 4)(2) = −16.

new w = old w − learning_rate × gradient
= 0 − 0.1 × (−16) = 1.6

The new prediction is 3.2 and the new loss is 0.64. The negative gradient told us to increase w, and the learning rate controlled how far.

Explore: the learning rate

Same model and starting point, 15 updates. The vertical axis uses a log scale, so each gridline is 100× smaller than the one above it.

Try 0.02 (slow), 0.1 (fast), 0.125 (exact in one step), 0.2 (overshoots back and forth), 0.25 (bounces forever) and 0.3 (diverges).

Backpropagation: gradients for every layer

With several layers, an early weight affects a hidden value, which affects the next layer, which affects the loss. The chain rule (A4) multiplies the local slopes along that path. Backpropagation applies the rule efficiently, starting at the loss and working backward. Each step reuses the product already computed for the layer above it.

Backprop by hand: two weights

Network: z = w₁x, h = ReLU(z), prediction = w₂h, L = (prediction − y)². We set x = 2, y = 2, w₁ = 0.5, w₂ = 1.

Forward: z = 1, h = 1, prediction = 1, L = (1 − 2)² = 1

dL/dpred = 2(pred − y) = −2
dL/dw₂ = dL/dpred × h = −2 × 1 = −2
dL/dw₁ = dL/dpred × w₂ × ReLU′(z) × x = (−2)(1)(1)(2) = −4

ReLU′(z) = 1 because z is positive. With learning rate 0.1: w₁ becomes 0.5 + 0.4 = 0.9, and w₂ becomes 1 + 0.2 = 1.2. The new forward pass gives h = 1.8, prediction 2.16 and loss 0.0256, much better.

Two common confusions

Backpropagation computes gradients. The optimizer uses them to update the weights. These are separate steps. Also, compute every gradient from the same old weights before updating any of them.

The training loop, and inference

Forward pass: compute predictions and the loss
↓
Backward pass: compute gradients with backprop
↓
Update: parameters −= learning_rate × gradient
↺
Repeat with the next batch of data

During inference, when the trained model is in use, only the forward pass runs, and the parameters stay fixed. Translating a new sentence changes the inputs and intermediate values, never W_Q or any other learned matrix.

Key takeaways

  • Update rule: w ← w − learning_rate × ∂L/∂w.
  • Backpropagation is the chain rule run backward from the loss. It gives every parameter's gradient in one pass.
  • The learning rate has to be tuned: too small is slow, too large oscillates or diverges.

Lesson B4 · Neural networks

Training in practice: batches, Adam and overfitting

⏱ About 20 minUses:B3Gradient descent and backprop

Goal: Know the practical machinery around gradient descent that the Transformer paper relies on: mini-batches, Adam, learning-rate schedules, dropout and validation data.

B3 updated one weight on one example. The paper trained 65 million parameters on 4.5 million sentence pairs. A few practical ideas bridge that gap, and each one appears by name in the paper's training section.

Mini-batches and epochs

Computing the exact gradient over the whole dataset before every step would be far too slow. Instead, each step uses a mini-batch: a random sample of examples whose average gradient approximates the full one. This is stochastic gradient descent. One full pass over the data is an epoch. The paper built batches of about 25,000 source tokens and 25,000 target tokens, grouping sentences of similar length so that little padding was wasted.

Adam: a smarter step

Plain gradient descent uses the same learning rate for every parameter. Adam keeps two running averages for each parameter. One is the average gradient, called momentum, which smooths out noisy batches. The other is the average squared gradient, which shrinks the step for parameters whose gradients are consistently large. The paper used Adam with β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.98 and ε = 10⁻⁹. Those three numbers control the two averages and prevent division by zero.

Learning-rate schedules and warmup

The learning rate doesn't have to be constant. Early in training the Adam statistics are unreliable and the gradients can be wild, so the paper warms up: it increases the learning rate linearly over the first 4,000 steps, then decays it slowly. E6 plots the exact formula.

Overfitting and how to detect it

A model can memorize its training data and still fail on new sentences. This is overfitting. To catch it, keep data aside: a validation set to monitor during training, and a test set touched only for the final result. When the training loss keeps falling but the validation loss rises, the model is memorizing.

Regularization: dropout, and a preview of label smoothing

Dropout randomly zeroes a fraction of activations at each training step, so the network can't rely on any single path. With rate 0.1, about 10% of values become 0, and the survivors are multiplied by 1/0.9 so that the average stays the same. At inference dropout is switched off. The paper applies dropout of 0.1 to every sublayer output and to the embeddings.

Dropout on one vector

Rate 0.5 (large, for visibility). [2, 4, 6, 8] with a random mask [1, 0, 1, 0] → [2, 0, 6, 0] × 2 = [4, 0, 12, 0]. On average over many masks, each entry still equals its original value.

Label smoothing (E6) trains against slightly softened targets so the model doesn't become overconfident. Other common tools include weight decay and early stopping.

Lesson C1 · Text and sequences

Tokenization: from text to IDs

⏱ About 15 minNo prerequisites: a good starting point.

Goal: Explain what a tokenizer does, run byte-pair encoding by hand, and know the special tokens a Transformer needs.

A network can't calculate with the word "tea". Text first has to become numbers, and that happens in two separate steps. A tokenizer chooses the pieces and gives each one an ID. An embedding (C2) gives each ID a vector. This lesson covers the first step.

Tokens and IDs

A tokenizer splits text into tokens and maps each token to its index in a fixed vocabulary. In a deliberately simple word-level example, "I like tea" becomes ["I", "like", "tea"] with IDs [0, 1, 2]. These IDs are addresses, not quantities: token 2 is not "twice" token 1.

Why not words, and why not characters?

Whole words make the vocabulary huge, and any unseen word ("teapots", a name, a typo) becomes an unknown token. Single characters never meet an unknown, but sentences become very long, and the model has to learn spelling before it can learn meaning. Subwords sit in between. Common words stay whole, and rare words split into reusable pieces.

Byte-pair encoding (BPE), by hand

BPE builds a subword vocabulary from data. Start with characters, then repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token. Take a tiny corpus of word counts: low ×5, lower ×2, newest ×6, widest ×3.

MergeMost frequent pairCountWhy
1e + s → es9newest (6) + widest (3)
2es + t → est9the same two words
3l + o → lo7low (5) + lower (2)
4lo + w → low7the same two words

Now tokenize a word the corpus never contained, lowest. Apply the merges in order: l o w e s t → l o w es t → l o w est → lo w est → low · est. Two meaningful pieces, and no unknown token. (Real BPE also marks word endings and runs tens of thousands of merges.)

Embeddings: from IDs to vectors

⏱ About 15 minUses:A2Matrices, shapes and transposeC1Tokenization

Goal: Understand embedding lookup, why it is a matrix product in disguise, and why a token's first vector is not its final meaning.

We have IDs. The network needs vectors. An embedding gives each vocabulary entry its own learned vector, and that is where the running example's numbers come from.

The embedding table

TokenIDToy embedding (row of E)
I0[1, 0]
like1[0, 1]

An embedding table E has one row per vocabulary entry. Looking up IDs [0, 1, 2] stacks rows 0, 1 and 2 into the (3, 2) matrix X you met in A2. These values are hand-picked for easy arithmetic. Real values start random and are learned.

The paper calls the vector width d_model = 512. With a 37,000-token vocabulary, E has shape (37000, 512), about 19 million numbers. A 7-token sentence becomes a (7, 512) input matrix.

A lookup is secretly a matrix product

Write token ID 2 as a one-hot vector: all zeros except a 1 at position 2, giving [0, 0, 1, 0]. Multiplying it by E selects row 2:

[0, 0, 1, 0] @ E = 0·[1,0] + 0·[0,1] + 1·[1,1] + 0·[1,0.8] = [1, 1] ← tea's row

So an embedding is just a linear layer (B1) applied to one-hot inputs, and it is trained by backprop like any other weight. Libraries skip the multiplication and index the row directly, because that is far faster. Only the rows for tokens in the current batch receive gradient updates.

Similar tokens end up nearby

Training moves vectors around so that tokens used in similar ways get similar vectors. In our toy table, tea [1,1] and coffee [1,0.8] are close: their cosine similarity (A1) is about 0.99. Individual dimensions usually have no clean human label such as "drinkable". The meaning is in the geometry, not in any single number.

The starting vector is not the final meaning

"Bank" gets the same embedding row in "river bank" and in "bank loan". A useful model must represent those two occurrences differently once it has read the context. The layers above the embedding build contextual representations (also called hidden states), and attention is the main tool for that. The jobs divide cleanly: tokenization identifies the pieces, embeddings give learned starting points, and the network transforms those starting points using context.

Paper detail (you'll see it again in E4): the Transformer multiplies embeddings by √d_model ≈ 22.6 before adding positional encodings, and shares one matrix between the source embedding, the target embedding and the final output layer.

Lesson C3 · Text and sequences

RNNs and the seq2seq bottleneck

⏱ About 15 minUses:B1Neurons and layersC2Embeddings

Goal: Describe how a recurrent encoder–decoder translates, and name the three problems the Transformer was designed to remove.

Imagine translating Ich mag Tee. A model must represent the source sentence, relate words that may be far apart, and generate a target sentence. Before 2017, the strongest systems did this with recurrent neural networks.

A recurrent network reads one token at a time

An RNN keeps a hidden state vector, a running summary. At each step it combines the current token's embedding with the previous state to produce the new state:

h_t = f(W_x x_t + W_h h_{t−1} + b) (f is a nonlinearity; LSTMs and GRUs are fancier versions)

Think of reading while updating a running note, except that the note is a learned vector of fixed size, however long the sentence is.

Encoder–decoder (seq2seq)

For translation, one RNN (the encoder) reads the source, and a second RNN (the decoder) generates the target, starting from the encoder's final state.

Three problems

  1. The bottleneck. A 50-word sentence and a 5-word sentence both have to fit into one fixed-size vector c. Long sentences lose detail.
  2. Long paths. Information from word 1 reaches word 40 only through 39 state updates. During training, gradients must travel the same long chain back, and they tend to shrink (the vanishing gradient problem), so long-range dependencies are hard to learn.
  3. Sequential computation. h_t can't be computed until h_{t−1} is done. Even when the whole sentence is known in advance, as it is in training, a GPU can't process the positions in parallel.

The next lesson shows how attention fixed problem 1. The Transformer then fixed problems 2 and 3 by removing recurrence entirely.

Key takeaways

  • An RNN updates a hidden state one token at a time. Seq2seq uses an encoder RNN followed by a decoder RNN.
  • The problems: one-vector bottleneck, long paths for information and gradients, and no parallelism across positions.

Lesson C4 · Text and sequences

Attention before Transformers

⏱ About 15 minUses:C3RNNs and the bottleneckA3Exponentials, logs and softmaxA1Dot product as similarity

Goal: See that attention was invented as an add-on to RNNs, and pin down exactly what the Transformer paper changed.

In 2014, Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio asked a simple question: why force the decoder to rely on one summary vector, when every encoder state h₁, h₂, h₃ is still available? Let the decoder look back at all of them, and choose differently at each step.

The mechanism

At each decoder step the model does three things:

  1. Score every encoder state against the current decoder state.
  2. Softmax the scores into weights (A3).
  3. Take the weighted average of the encoder states. The result is a context vector made fresh for this step.

This removes the bottleneck. Nothing has to be squeezed into one vector any more, because each output step builds its own mixture. Translation of long sentences improved substantially. You will build exactly this weighted-average idea in D1.

What was still missing

The encoder and decoder were still RNNs. Attention connected them, but the long paths within each sentence and the step-by-step computation (C3, problems 2 and 3) remained.

The Transformer's move

The Transformer paper did not invent attention. Its proposal was to build the whole sequence model out of attention and drop recurrence and convolution entirely. The new ingredient that makes this possible is self-attention: each position in a sentence attends to the other positions in the same sentence. In one layer, word 1 and word 40 interact directly. When the sequence is known, as in training, every position's calculation can run in parallel.

There is a tradeoff. Full self-attention scores every pair of positions, so its cost grows with n² for a sequence of length n. For sentence-length inputs this is cheap. For very long documents it becomes the main cost (G2).

Read the title in context

"Attention Is All You Need" doesn't mean the model contains only attention. It also has embeddings, feed-forward layers, residual connections, normalization and an output projection. The title means attention is all you need for mixing information between positions, with no recurrence.

Lesson D1 · Attention, one piece at a time

Attention as a weighted average

⏱ About 15 minUses:A1Dot product as similarityC4Attention before Transformers

Goal: Understand attention's output (a context-dependent weighted mixture of vectors) before worrying about where the weights come from.

In "river bank", the representation of "bank" should absorb some information from "river". Attention is the operation that gathers information from relevant positions. For this lesson, assume someone hands us the weights, and look only at what the operation does with them.

Start with a plain average, then improve it

Suppose three positions offer the vectors v₁ = [2, 0], v₂ = [0, 2] and v₃ = [2, 2]. (These are exactly twice our token vectors. D3 explains why the vectors being mixed are a separate, learned copy.) A plain average gives each position weight ⅓. But different positions should contribute different amounts, depending on which token is doing the gathering.

Attention uses a weighted average. With weights [0.2, 0.3, 0.5]:

0.2·[2,0] + 0.3·[0,2] + 0.5·[2,2]
= [0.4, 0] + [0, 0.6] + [1, 1]
= [1.4, 1.6]

The output is a new vector that blends information from all three. It isn't the list of weights, and it isn't the index of the largest weight. If the weights were [1, 0, 0], the output would simply be v₁. With [⅓, ⅓, ⅓] it would be the plain average.

The weights must depend on who is asking

"Bank" needs a different mixture from "river", even within the same sentence. So each position computes its own weights: compatibility scores between itself and every candidate position, turned into weights by softmax. That is the next lesson. The result is one mixture per position, and every position is updated at the same time.

The "river bank" story explains the motivation. It isn't a rule anyone writes into the network. Useful patterns are learned from the training objective.

Keep these two things separate

Attention weights say how much each position contributes to one mixture. They are non-negative and sum to 1. The attention output is the weighted sum of vectors. A large weight isn't a claim that a word is "important" in a human sense, and it isn't a complete explanation of the model's decision.

  • Attention output = Σ weightᵢ × vectorᵢ, a blend whose width equals the vectors' width.
  • Every position gets its own weights, so every position gets its own blend.
  • Weights describe the mixture. The output is the information actually passed on.

Lesson D3 · Attention, one piece at a time

Why queries, keys and values are separate

⏱ About 20 minUses:A2Matrices, shapes and transposeD2Scores to weights, by hand

Goal: Explain what Q, K and V are, how they're computed, and what problem the separate projections solve.

In D2 each token's vector did three jobs at once. The Transformer splits them up: three learned matrices turn each token into three different vectors, one per job.

Three jobs, three vectors

VectorJobSearch analogy
Query qwhat this token is looking forthe search request you type
Key kwhat this token offers for matchinga document's index terms
Value vwhat this token hands over if chosenthe document's content

The scores become query · key, and the mixture is a weighted sum of values. In the model these are numeric vectors, not literal questions or documents. The analogy only describes the roles.

Q = X W_Q K = X W_K V = X W_V

X holds the token vectors, one per row. W_Q, W_K and W_V are learned parameter matrices, fixed after training. Q, K and V change with every input. In the base model each of these matrices is (512, 64) per head, so each token's query, key and value have 64 numbers (D5 explains why 64).

Separate matrices break the symmetry

The score table is QKᵀ = X W_Q W_Kᵀ Xᵀ. If W_Q = W_K, it is symmetric: A's score for B always equals B's score for A. With different matrices, it doesn't have to be. Try W_Q = [[1,1],[0,1]] (which maps [a, b] to [a, a + b]) and keep W_K as the identity:

Q = [[1,1], [0,1], [1,2]] K = X = [[1,0], [0,1], [1,1]]

QKᵀ = [[1, 1, 2], ← I's scores for I, like, tea
[0, 1, 1], ← like's scores
[1, 2, 3]] ← tea's scores

Now I → like scores 1, but like → I scores 0. The relationship has a direction. Training finds whatever directions turn out to be useful.

Separate values decouple "matching" from "content"

What makes a token easy to find can differ from what it should contribute once found. W_V lets the model pass along different features from the ones used for matching.

The running example from here on

To keep the arithmetic readable, the rest of Part D uses W_Q = W_K = identity and W_V = 2 × identity, so Q = K = X and V = 2X = [[2,0],[0,2],[2,2]]. Those are D1's values. Real projections are learned and far less tidy.

Why divide by √dₖ?

From A5: if the query and key entries are roughly random with variance 1, then q · k has variance dₖ, and its typical size is √dₖ. In the base model dₖ = 64, so raw scores typically land around ±8. Softmax (A3, property 4) turns score gaps that large into nearly one-hot weights: one position takes almost everything.

That's bad for learning. When softmax is saturated, its gradients are tiny, so the model barely learns which positions should matter. The paper puts it directly: for large dₖ, dot products grow large and push softmax into regions with extremely small gradients. Dividing by √dₖ brings the typical score back to about 1, whatever the width.

Lesson D5 · Attention, one piece at a time

Multi-head attention

⏱ About 20 minUses:D4Matrix form and the √dₖ scale

Goal: Understand how several attention heads run in parallel, how their outputs are combined, and why 8 heads of width 64 cost about the same as one head of width 512.

One attention head gives each query one set of weights, which is one way of deciding what matters. But a token may need several kinds of information at once: which noun a pronoun refers to, what the neighbouring words are, what the verb's object is. Multi-head attention gives the model several independent ways to look.

Each head has its own projections

Head i has its own W_Qⁱ, W_Kⁱ and W_Vⁱ. The same input therefore produces different queries, keys and values in each head, and so different attention patterns. Each head runs the D4 equation independently.

A learned pattern might help link a pronoun to its noun, while another tracks the previous word. These are interpretations researchers sometimes find, not jobs assigned by the programmer. Heads can also learn overlapping or hard-to-read behaviour.

headᵢ = Attention(X W_Qⁱ, X W_Kⁱ, X W_Vⁱ)
MultiHead(X) = Concat(head₁, …, head_h) W_O

Follow the shapes

StageSmall example (3 tokens, width 8, 2 heads)Base model (n tokens)
Input X(3, 8)(n, 512)
Each head's Q, K, V(3, 4)(n, 64)
Each head's weight table(3, 3)(n, n)
Each head's output(3, 4)(n, 64)
Concatenate the heads' features(3, 8)(n, 512)
Multiply by W_O(3, 8)(n, 512)

Concatenation places the heads' features side by side for each token. It doesn't add tokens. W_O then mixes information across heads.

Why 64? Splitting the width keeps the cost flat

The paper sets dₖ = dᵥ = d_model / h = 512 / 8 = 64. Eight 64-wide heads have the same projection sizes as one 512-wide head: 8 × (512 × 64) = 512 × 512. So multi-head attention costs about the same as single-head attention, and buys eight distinct attention patterns per position. The paper found this worked better than one wide head. Too few or too many heads both hurt slightly.

In practice the heads aren't computed one after another. A single (512, 512) matrix produces all eight heads' queries at once, and the result is split into eight 64-wide slices that are processed side by side. The same happens for keys and values.

What heads don't do

Heads don't divide the sentence into groups of words. Each head attends over all allowed positions. What heads split is the feature dimension: each works in its own 64-wide projected space.

Lesson D6 · Attention, one piece at a time

Masks: padding and causal

⏱ About 25 minUses:D4Matrix form and the √dₖ scaleA3Exponentials, logs and softmax

Goal: Apply a padding mask and a causal mask to attention scores, and explain why the decoder needs the causal one.

Sometimes a position must not attend somewhere. It may be filler that only exists to make a batch rectangular. Or it may be a word the model is supposed to predict, which it must not be allowed to see. Both cases use the same trick, applied to the scores before softmax.

The trick: −∞ before softmax

Set a forbidden score to −∞. Since exp(−∞) = 0, that position gets weight exactly 0, and softmax renormalizes the remaining weights so they still sum to 1. (Zeroing weights after softmax would leave rows summing to less than 1.) In practice, implementations use a very large negative number such as −10⁹ instead of a literal −∞.

Padding masks

Batches need equal-length sequences, so short sentences are filled with PAD tokens (C1). Take a batch of Ich mag Tee and Hallo, padded to "Hallo PAD PAD". A padding mask sets every score toward a PAD key to −∞, so no real token gathers information from filler. The outputs computed at PAD positions are simply ignored, and the loss skips them.

Padding masks are used everywhere: in encoder self-attention, in decoder self-attention, and on the source side of cross-attention.

Causal masks: no peeking at the answer

During training, the decoder receives the whole target sentence, shifted right by one position, and must predict the next token at every position:

Decoder inputs: [START, I, like, tea ]
Labels: [I, like, tea, END ]

The input at position "like" must predict "tea". If attention let it see the next input, which is "tea" itself, the task would be trivial copying, and the model would fail at generation time, when the future really is unknown. The causal mask lets position i attend only to positions ≤ i. That makes the allowed region of the score table a lower triangle.

"like" is part of the known prefix. The next token, "tea", is the label for that position. Letting the position see the later input "tea" would reveal the answer it is being trained to predict.

Key takeaways

  • Masking means setting forbidden scores to −∞ before softmax, which gives those positions zero weight.
  • Padding masks hide filler. Causal masks hide future positions in the decoder.
  • The causal mask is what makes parallel training on whole target sentences honest.

Lesson D7 · Attention, one piece at a time

Positional encoding

⏱ About 20 minUses:C2EmbeddingsD4Matrix form and the √dₖ scale

Goal: Understand why attention needs position information, and how the paper's sinusoidal encoding supplies it.

"Dog bites person" and "Person bites dog" contain the same words but describe very different events. Everything we've built so far would treat them identically.

Attention alone ignores order

Look at D4 again. Nothing in softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)V refers to position. If you shuffle the rows of X, the output rows shuffle in exactly the same way, and each token gets the same vector as before. Without extra information, the model sees a set of tokens, not a sequence.

The fix: add a position vector to each embedding

The Transformer adds a positional encoding PE(pos) to each token's embedding. It has the same width (d_model), so the shape doesn't change. The same word at two positions now enters the network as two different vectors.

The paper's sinusoids

PE(pos, 2i) = sin( pos / 10000^(2i / d_model) )
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos( pos / 10000^(2i / d_model) )

Each pair of dimensions is a sine and cosine wave of a different frequency. The first pairs turn quickly, and later pairs turn more and more slowly. Think of a clock's second, minute and hour hands: the combined reading identifies a moment uniquely, and nearby moments have similar readings.

The running example, with d_model = 2

With only one pair (i = 0), the denominator is 1, so PE(pos) = [sin(pos), cos(pos)].

pos 0, I: [1, 0] + [0.000, 1.000] = [1.000, 1.000]
pos 1, like: [0, 1] + [0.841, 0.540] = [0.841, 1.540]
pos 2, tea: [1, 1] + [0.909, −0.416] = [1.909, 0.584]

The real model first multiplies each embedding by √d_model, which we skip here to keep the numbers readable. Notice that "I" at position 0 now equals tea's raw embedding [1, 1]. With 2 dimensions, collisions like that are easy. With 512, they essentially never happen.

Explore: the encoding for 50 positions, d_model = 64

Each row is one position and each column is one dimension. The left columns change fast and the right columns change slowly. Pick a position to see how similar its encoding is to every other position's.

−1+1rows: position 0 → 49 · columns: dimension 0 → 63

Why sinusoids?

  • Relative positions are easy to use. For any fixed offset k, PE(pos + k) is a linear function of PE(pos). It is a rotation of each sine/cosine pair. The paper hypothesized that this lets the model learn "attend 3 tokens back" easily.
  • No length limit is built in. The formula works for any position, even ones longer than any training sentence.
  • Learned position vectors worked about as well. The paper tried them and got nearly identical results, then kept sinusoids for the extrapolation argument.

Later models often use other schemes, such as learned positions, relative positions or rotary embeddings (G2). The need is the same, and you now know what it is.

Key takeaways

  • Self-attention is blind to order. Positional encodings add that information.
  • The paper adds sine/cosine waves of many frequencies, one value per dimension, to each embedding.
  • Masking controls what can be seen. Positional encoding says where things are.

Lesson E1 · The Transformer

Feed-forward, residual connections and layer normalization

⏱ About 20 minUses:B1Neurons and layersA5Mean, variance and normalizationD4Matrix form and the √dₖ scale

Goal: Understand the three supporting components that turn attention into a block you can stack: what each one computes, and why it is there.

Attention moves information between positions. A Transformer block needs three more things: a way to process each token's gathered information, a way to keep signals and gradients flowing through many layers, and a way to keep numbers in a stable range. Those are the feed-forward network, residual connections and layer normalization.

1 · The position-wise feed-forward network

This is the same small two-layer network (B1) applied to each token's vector separately, with the same weights at every position:

FFN(x) = ReLU(x W₁ + b₁) W₂ + b₂

In the base model W₁ is (512, 2048) and W₂ is (2048, 512): the network widens each token four times, applies ReLU, and narrows it back. A toy version with 2 → 3 → 2:

Worked example
x = [1, −1] W₁ = [[1, 0, 1], W₂ = [[1, 0 ],
[0, 1, −1]] [0, 1 ],
[0.5, 0.5]] (biases 0)

x W₁ = [1, −1, 2] → ReLU → [1, 0, 2] → W₂ → [1·1 + 2·0.5, 0 + 2·0.5] = [2, 1]

The division of labour is simple: attention mixes across tokens, and the FFN transforms features within a token. About two-thirds of an encoder block's parameters are in the FFN.

2 · Residual connections

Instead of replacing its input, each sublayer adds its output to the input: x + Sublayer(x). For the running example's "like", the input [0, 1] plus the attention output from D4, [1.198, 1.604], gives [1.198, 2.604].

Why it helps: each sublayer only has to learn a correction to what's already there, and there is always a direct path from the output back to the input. During backprop (B3), gradients flow along that path without being multiplied through every layer's weights, which makes deep stacks trainable. The shapes must match, which is why every sublayer outputs d_model features.

3 · Layer normalization

Layer normalization normalizes each token vector across its own features (A5), then applies a learned scale γ and shift β:

LayerNorm(x) = γ · (x − mean(x)) / √(var(x) + ε) + β
Worked example

x = [1, 2, 3, 6]: mean 3, variance 3.5, standard deviation ≈ 1.871.

(x − 3) / 1.871 ≈ [−1.069, −0.535, 0, 1.604] (then × γ + β, which start at 1 and 0)

With only two features, the result is always [−1, 1], [1, −1] or [0, 0]. "like"'s [1.198, 2.604] becomes [−1, 1]. That is a quirk of tiny vectors, and one more reason real models use 512.

The ε is a tiny constant that prevents division by zero. Unlike softmax, LayerNorm's outputs can be negative and don't sum to anything in particular.

How they combine: Add & Norm

The paper wraps every sublayer the same way, with dropout (B4) applied to the sublayer's output:

output = LayerNorm( x + Dropout(Sublayer(x)) )

This is "post-LN": normalize after adding. Many later models use "pre-LN", x + Sublayer(LayerNorm(x)), which trains more stably in very deep stacks (G2). When reading other code, check which one it uses.

Key takeaways

  • FFN: the same two-layer ReLU network applied to each token (512 → 2048 → 512).
  • Residual: x + F(x), so sublayers learn corrections and gradients get a direct path back.
  • LayerNorm normalizes each token's features. The paper applies LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x)) around every sublayer.

Lesson E2 · The Transformer

The encoder block and stack

⏱ About 15 minUses:D5Multi-head attentionD6Padding and causal masksD7Positional encodingE1Feed-forward, residuals, layer norm

Goal: Draw one encoder block from memory, and explain what the six-block stack produces.

The encoder's job is to turn the source sentence into contextual vectors: one vector per source token that reflects the whole sentence. Every component is now familiar. This lesson puts them in the paper's order.

The block, step by step

  1. Input: source embeddings (C2), multiplied by √d_model, plus positional encodings (D7). Shape (n, 512).
  2. Multi-head self-attention (D5) over the whole source. No causal mask, because the source is fully known. A padding mask applies if there are PAD tokens.
  3. Add & Norm (E1).
  4. Feed-forward (E1), applied to each position.
  5. Add & Norm again.

Stacking: N = 6

The paper stacks six identical blocks, each with its own parameters. The shape stays (n, 512) all the way up, so blocks can be stacked freely, but the vectors grow more contextual with each block. Early blocks tend to capture local and surface patterns, later ones more abstract relationships. These are tendencies, not rules.

The final output is called the memory: one 512-number vector per source token. For Ich mag Tee that's a (3, 512) matrix. It is not a translation. It is a representation that the decoder will consult through cross-attention at every step.

The diagram omits dropout, which the paper applies to each sublayer's output and to the embedding sum during training. It's switched off at inference.

The decoder: masked self-attention and cross-attention

⏱ About 25 minUses:D6Padding and causal masksE2The encoder

Goal: Explain the decoder's three sublayers, and exactly where cross-attention gets its queries, keys and values.

The decoder has two jobs at every position: understand the target words produced so far, and decide which source words matter for the next word. It therefore needs two kinds of attention.

Three sublayers instead of two

Target embeddings (shifted right) × √d_model + positions
↓
Masked multi-head self-attention → Add & NormD6
↓
Multi-head cross-attention to the encoder memory → Add & NormE3
↓
Feed-forward → Add & NormE1

Masked self-attention is D6: target positions attend to themselves and earlier target positions only. The feed-forward sublayer is E1. The new piece is the middle one.

Cross-attention

Cross-attention is the D4 equation with its inputs coming from two places:

Comes fromShape (4 target positions, 3 source tokens)
Queries Qthe decoder (output of its masked self-attention), × W_Q(4, 64) per head
Keys Kthe encoder memory, × W_K(3, 64)
Values Vthe encoder memory, × W_V(3, 64)
Weightssoftmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)(4, 3): target rows × source columns
Outputweights @ V(4, 64)

Each target position asks "given what I've produced so far, which source words do I need now?" The answer is a mixture of source information. This is the 2014 idea from C4, rebuilt with Transformer attention. There is no causal mask here: the whole source may always be consulted. Only source padding is masked.

Illustrative cross-attention weights, like those a trained model might produce. Rows are decoder inputs and columns are source tokens.

Look at the pattern. The row for input "like" focuses on Tee, because position "like" is predicting the next word, "tea". Each row looks at the source word needed for the following target word. The weights are invented for illustration. Real heads are messier, and different heads attend differently.

Six decoder blocks, one shared memory

The paper stacks six decoder blocks. Every one of them cross-attends to the same final encoder output, not to the encoder block at the same depth. After the sixth block, a linear layer and softmax turn each position's vector into next-token probabilities (E4).

Training versus generation

In training, the whole shifted target is known, so all four positions are computed in parallel and the causal mask keeps the computation honest. In generation, the model has only the tokens it has produced so far: it predicts one, appends it, and runs again (E7).

Lesson E4 · The Transformer

The output layer: from vectors to next-token probabilities

⏱ About 15 minUses:A3Exponentials, logs and softmaxB2Loss functionsC2Embeddings

Goal: Compute next-token probabilities from a decoder vector, and understand the paper's weight sharing.

After six decoder blocks, each target position has a 512-number vector. A prediction needs one probability for each of the ~37,000 vocabulary tokens. Two familiar operations bridge the gap: a linear layer and softmax.

Linear → logits → softmax

logits = h W_out + b (512) @ (512, 37000) → (37000) scores
probabilities = softmax(logits)

This is the classifier from B2, with one class per vocabulary token. It is applied at every target position.

Weight tying: reuse the embedding table

The paper shares one matrix between the source embedding, the target embedding and this output layer. Concretely, W_out = Eᵀ. So the logit for token j is the dot product (A1) between the decoder's vector h and token j's embedding. The model scores each candidate word by how well h aligns with that word's vector. Sharing also saves about 19 million parameters per copy. (The shared English–German BPE vocabulary from C1 is what makes this possible.)

Because the same matrix is used both ways, the paper multiplies embeddings by √d_model on the input side, so that the embeddings stay in proportion to the positional encodings added to them.

Worked example with a 5-token vocabulary

Embedding rows: END = [−1, −1], I = [1, 0], like = [0, 1], tea = [1, 1], coffee = [1, 0.8]. The final decoder vector at position "like" is h = [1.2, 1.1].

logits = h · each row = [−2.30, 1.20, 1.10, 2.30, 2.08]
softmax ≈ [0.004, 0.136, 0.123, 0.409, 0.328] ← tea most likely

"coffee" comes a close second, because its embedding is close to tea's (C2). If the label is "tea", the loss is −ln 0.409 ≈ 0.895 (B2).

Lesson E6 · The Transformer

How the Transformer is trained

⏱ About 25 minUses:B4Training in practiceD6Padding and causal masksE4The output layer

Goal: Describe one training step of the paper's model, including teacher forcing, the learning-rate schedule and label smoothing.

Part B covered general training. This lesson covers the specific recipe in the paper's Section 5, which the first-reading guide in E8 tells you to leave for later. You now have everything needed to read it.

Teacher forcing: train every position at once

The decoder receives the correct target, shifted right ([START, I, like, tea]), rather than its own guesses. This is teacher forcing. Combined with the causal mask (D6), every target position is a separate next-token prediction, and all of them are computed in one parallel pass. A 30-token sentence gives 30 training signals in one step. This is a big part of why Transformers train so much faster than RNNs.

One consequence: in training, the model always sees a perfect prefix. At generation time it sees its own possibly imperfect output. The mismatch is called exposure bias. It's usually tolerable, but worth knowing about.

The loss

Cross-entropy (B2) at every non-PAD target position, averaged. Padding positions are excluded through the same mask idea as D6.

The learning-rate schedule

lrate = d_model^(−0.5) · min( step^(−0.5), step · warmup_steps^(−1.5) )

For the first warmup_steps (4,000), the second term is smaller, so the rate grows linearly. After that, the first term is smaller, so the rate decays in proportion to 1/√step. The peak is at step 4,000: 512^(−0.5) × 4000^(−0.5) ≈ 0.0007.

Explore: the warmup schedule

Why warm up? At the start, the weights are random and Adam's running averages (B4) are still empty, so early gradients are unreliable. Large steps then can push a post-LN Transformer into a bad region from which it never recovers. Starting small and ramping up avoids that.

Label smoothing

Instead of a hard target (100% on the right token), the paper trains against a softened target with ε_ls = 0.1. In the common formulation, the target keeps 1 − ε of its mass on the correct token and spreads ε evenly across the vocabulary:

vocabulary of 4, correct = token 2: hard [0, 0, 1, 0] → smoothed [0.025, 0.025, 0.925, 0.025]

This discourages the model from becoming extremely confident. The paper notes it hurts perplexity, because the model learns to be more unsure, but improves accuracy and BLEU.

How it generates: greedy decoding and beam search

⏱ About 20 minUses:E4The output layerE3The decoder and cross-attention

Goal: Run the generation loop by hand, and show with an example why beam search can beat greedy decoding.

A trained model outputs a probability for every possible next token. To translate, we still have to choose tokens, one at a time, feeding each choice back in. The way we choose matters.

The autoregressive loop

  1. Run the encoder once on Ich mag Tee to get the memory.
  2. Start the decoder with [START].
  3. Run the decoder, take the last position's probabilities, and choose a token.
  4. Append it: [START, I]. Repeat step 3.
  5. Stop when END is chosen, or at a maximum length. The paper uses input length + 50.

This is why generation is sequential even though training is parallel: token t+1 can't be chosen until token t exists.

Greedy decoding and its trap

Greedy decoding picks the single most likely token at every step. It's fast, but a locally best choice can lead to a worse sentence overall.

Greedy takes "The" (0.5 > 0.4) and ends with "The dog" at 0.20. But "A dog" has probability 0.36. Greedy never looked down that path.

Beam search

Beam search keeps the k best partial sequences (the "beam") at every step. It extends each one by every possible token and keeps the k best of the results. With k = 2 in the example: keep "The" (0.5) and "A" (0.4), expand both, and find "A dog" = 0.36 as the winner. In practice, implementations add log-probabilities rather than multiplying probabilities, to avoid tiny numbers.

Products of probabilities shrink with every token, so raw beam search favours short outputs. A length penalty normalizes for that. The paper used beam size 4 and length penalty α = 0.6.

Sampling and temperature (modern chat models)

Translation wants the most likely output. Open-ended text often wants variety. Sampling draws the next token at random according to the probabilities. Temperature divides the logits before softmax. That is exactly the scale effect from A3: below 1 is sharper and more predictable, above 1 is flatter and more varied. Top-k and top-p sampling restrict the draw to the most likely tokens.

A note on cost

Done naively, each new token re-runs the decoder over the whole prefix. But the keys and values of earlier positions never change, so they can be stored and reused. This KV cache (G2) is standard in every modern system.

Lesson G1 · After the paper

Encoder-only, decoder-only and encoder–decoder

⏱ About 15 minUses:E2The encoderE3The decoder and cross-attentionD6Padding and causal masks

Goal: Place BERT, GPT and T5 relative to the 2017 design, and know which family suits which job.

Within two years of the paper, researchers found that you don't always need both halves. Keep only the encoder, keep only the decoder, or keep both. Each choice changes the attention mask and the training objective, and so what the model is good at.

Encoder-only

Every token sees every token (bidirectional).

Decoder-only

Each token sees only earlier tokens (causal, D6).

Encoder–decoder

Bidirectional over the input; causal over the output, plus cross-attention.

Each grid shows which positions (columns) a position (row) may attend to. Filled cells are allowed.

Encoder-only: BERT (2018)

BERT keeps just the encoder stack. Because every token sees both left and right context, it can't be trained on "predict the next word", which would be trivial when the next word is visible. Instead it uses masked language modelling: hide about 15% of the input tokens and predict them from the surrounding context. BERT-style models excel at understanding tasks such as classification, named-entity tagging and producing embeddings for search. They don't generate long text naturally.

Decoder-only: GPT (2018 onward)

GPT keeps the decoder stack and removes cross-attention, since there is no encoder to consult. Each block is masked self-attention followed by the FFN. Training is plain next-token prediction on huge amounts of text, using exactly the teacher forcing and causal mask from E6. The prompt and the response are simply one sequence: the model reads the prompt and keeps predicting the next token (E7). Almost all of today's chat assistants are decoder-only.

Encoder–decoder: the original, and T5 (2019)

T5 kept the full 2017 structure and framed every task as text-to-text: "translate: …", "summarize: …", "question: …". Encoder–decoder models remain a natural fit when there is a distinct input to transform, as in translation or summarization.

  • Decoder-only (GPT): causal, trained on next-token prediction, good at generation. This is the basis of modern LLMs.
  • Encoder–decoder (original, T5): a natural fit for input → output transformations.

Four changes worth understanding properly

Pre-LN. In the original, every residual sum passes through a LayerNorm, so the "highway" from output to input is interrupted at every block. Pre-LN normalizes only the sublayer's input and leaves the residual sum untouched. Gradients get an uninterrupted path, deep models train reliably, and the strict need for warmup (E6) is reduced.

Rotary position embeddings (RoPE). In D7 you saw that a fixed offset corresponds to a rotation of each sine/cosine pair. RoPE takes that idea literally. Instead of adding a position vector, it rotates each query and key pair of dimensions by an angle proportional to the token's position. The dot product q·k then depends on the difference between positions, which is exactly the relative information attention needs.

The KV cache. During generation (E7), the keys and values of earlier tokens never change, because the causal mask stops later tokens from affecting them. So they are computed once and stored. Each new token computes only its own query, key and value, then attends over the cache. Generation becomes far cheaper per token, at the cost of memory that grows with context length. That is the motivation for sharing K and V across heads (GQA).

The n² problem. The (n × n) score table (D4) dominates for long inputs: doubling the context quadruples the attention work. FlashAttention makes the exact computation much faster in practice. Other approaches, such as sliding-window or sparse attention, restrict which pairs are scored.

Lesson G3 · After the paper

Pretraining, fine-tuning and instruction tuning

⏱ About 15 minUses:G1BERT, GPT and T5E6How it's trained

Goal: Understand the stages that turn a Transformer into a useful model, and close the loop on everything this course covered.

The 2017 model was trained from scratch for one task, translation, on labelled sentence pairs. Modern practice splits training into stages. A general model is pretrained once on enormous amounts of unlabelled text, then adapted cheaply to many uses.

Stage 1 · Pretraining

Train on a vast text corpus with a self-supervised objective: next-token prediction for decoder-only models, or masked tokens for encoder-only ones (G1). The labels come for free, because the text itself is the answer key. Through this one objective, the model absorbs grammar, facts and patterns of reasoning, because predicting the next word well requires them. The mechanics are exactly E6: teacher forcing, the causal mask and cross-entropy, at vastly larger scale.

Stage 2 · Fine-tuning

Continue training the pretrained model on a much smaller, task-specific labelled dataset, such as sentiment labels or medical question–answer pairs. Starting from pretrained weights needs far less data than training from scratch.

Stage 3 · Instruction tuning and preference tuning

A model pretrained only on next-token prediction continues text. It doesn't necessarily follow requests. Instruction tuning fine-tunes it on examples of instructions paired with good responses. Preference tuning then teaches it which of two responses people prefer. One method is RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, which trains a reward model on human comparisons and then optimizes against it. Another is DPO, direct preference optimization, which learns from the comparison pairs directly.

In-context learning: adapting without training

Large pretrained models can often perform a new task just from instructions or a few examples written in the prompt, with no parameter update at all. This fits B3's distinction between training and inference: the parameters stay fixed, and only the input changes. All the adaptation happens through attention over the prompt.
